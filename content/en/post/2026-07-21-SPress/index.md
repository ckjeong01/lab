Professor Tay Jeong of the Department of Sociology analyzed the characteristics of hashtag activism that unfolded in the global online space during the early phase of the Gaza War, using country-level X (formerly Twitter) trend data and a novel role-based analytical framework. The study has drawn significant academic attention for offering a clear and elegant theoretical framework to illuminate the dynamics of war-related public opinion on social media.

The Gaza War, which broke out in October 2023, sparked widespread political controversy from the very beginning. The author took as his starting point a debate among prominent progressive intellectuals worldwide over the symmetry of the war. While the claim that both sides of the conflict were driven by primal vengeance was widely advanced by mainstream Western intellectuals, a counter-argument held that Palestinian resistance should be understood not as an expression of hatred or vengeance, but as an expression of hope for liberation.

To address this question, Professor Jeong introduced a triadic model of three core roles that fundamentally structure political rhetoric — Victim, Villain, and Hero — and meticulously classified and analyzed war-related hashtag slogans appearing in X trends across 62 countries according to this three-role model.

Pro-Palestine hashtag activism worldwide was overwhelmingly dominated by voices calling attention to the suffering of victims and appealing for peace (#GazaUnderAttack, #CeasefireNOW). However, differences in modes of expression emerged depending on where users were located. While users in Western countries such as Europe and the United States focused on humanitarian sympathy — grieving over victims' suffering and calling for an end to armed conflict — users in Arab-majority countries in the Middle East strongly expressed heroism slogans, emphasizing courage and resistance alongside the victim frame.

Although such differences were commonly conceptualized as a West versus non-West divide, the study confirmed that, at least with respect to the current Gaza War, the more significant divide is between non-Arab and Arab countries. Contrary to the perception that the global pro-Palestine movement is a manifestation of primal vengeance or antisemitism, slogans condemning or attacking Israel appeared very infrequently, and militant support for Palestine was mostly expressed through the lens of heroism.

In contrast, pro-Israel hashtag activism was centered on villain-denunciation messages targeting the other side (#HamasTerrorists), with content emphasizing heroes being almost entirely absent — a distinctive pattern. Professor Jeong explained that in conflict situations marked by a significant power differential, the weaker side tends to seek the image of a hero in order to sustain hope, while the stronger side tends to focus on denouncing the other side in order to justify its own actions.

Professor Jeong stated: "Since the outbreak of the Gaza War, longstanding questions relating to violence by the colonized — including the symmetry and asymmetry of political rhetoric, humanitarianism versus anti-imperialism, and the gap between the West and non-West — have been actively debated, yet conceptual systematization has been lacking and empirical verification has been insufficient. I hope this study contributes, even in a small way, to resolving misunderstandings about the global pro-Palestine movement."
